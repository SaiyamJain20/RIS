**Verdict (final; unchanged after T2, T8 and the final search sweep): REFRAME** (confidence: medium).
Reasoning against the kill rule. No single work satisfies SQ1+SQ2+SQ3 together with SQ5 or SQ6 in a healthcare multi-institution setting, so A is **not DEAD**. But SQ1+SQ2 (+ partly SQ3) ARE satisfied by a whole line of work: SAP patent US10380366 (2017), Yang et al. 2018 (Hyperledger), Zhao et al. 2020, Islam et al. 2022, and the Mangala et al. FGCS 2026 healthcare design that "invokes the Privacy Budget Ledger smart contract to verify remaining budget sufficiency" before every DP query. So the idea "a ledger gates DP queries by a cumulative budget" is **not novel and must not be presented as the contribution**. SQ5 (Sybil queriers, global budget, depletion DoS) is covered only outside ledgers/custodians by Big Bird; SQ6 (same patient at several institutions) is stated as a problem by Trask-Prakash 2021 (with the exact two-hospital overlapping-patient example) and Rahimian et al. 2026, solved only for FL by ULDP-FL. Nobody combines them with MPC-based multi-custodian release + ledger gate + soundness definition + measured attacks. A reviewer WILL say "Zhao/Yang/SAP + Big Bird + Shrinkwrap/Drynx" (composition of known parts); the paper has to pre-empt this.
**3 closest threats:** (1) Mangala et al., FGCS 176 (2026) 108548 / arXiv 2512.10426v3 - healthcare consortium, Fabric, pre-query Privacy Budget Ledger contract (design only, no multi-custodian MPC, no overlap, no Sybil). (2) Zhao et al., IEEE IoT-J 2020 (+ Yang 2018, SAP patent 2017: same family) - ledger rejects queries when budget is exhausted (single curator). (3) Tholoniat et al., Big Bird, SOSP 2026 - global budget across untrusted queriers, Sybil depletion, formal unsoundness of per-querier enforcement (web advertising, no ledger/custodians). Also near: Trask-Prakash 2021 (SQ6 in a hospital example).
**Minimum publishable delta:** The contribution must be the part nobody has: (a) a **sound global accounting rule across mutually distrusting custodians with NO trusted curator**: patient-level (not custodian-level) budgets when the same patient is at several hospitals, plus per-issuing-authority accounting against Sybil identities from independent authorities, where each custodian independently verifies the committed debit before contributing MPC shares; (b) a **budget-soundness definition + proof** and **measured attacks that break the prior-art baselines** (per-custodian budgets, per-querier budgets, a single-chain counter as in Zhao/Yang/Islam): cross-silo overlap, hospital-subset differencing, Sybil GIDs, receipt replay, depletion DoS; (c) an honest **utility cost** analysis (see T10). Frame the ledger as the enforcement substrate, not the novelty, and cite SAP patent, Yang 2018, Zhao 2020, Islam 2022, Mangala 2026, Big Bird, Trask-Prakash, ULDP-FL, DProvDB, Drynx, Shrinkwrap explicitly.

**Conceptual flags (helper's judgement, not verified against the cited papers; flagged only, no redesign):**
1. *Keyed-hash patient groups (G=16)*: sound only if all hospitals compute the same group for the same person; a mistyped/missing/differently formatted ID puts one person in two groups (loss up to ~2B) and breaks the PSI weights. A full-population query debits all 16 groups (same cost as G=1 plus 16 debits of gas); the benefit exists only for strict-subset queries, i.e. a 1/16 random subsample whose answer must be rescaled (variance roughly x256). Low-entropy national IDs let any holder of the shared HMAC key brute-force membership (leaks membership, not soundness).
2. *Multiplicity-capped weights from pairwise PSI*: m(P) is fixed per data epoch; a new record at another site mid-epoch breaks sum(w) <= 1 unless a dataVersion rule forces re-PSI (not stated). Answers become weighted counts (a patient recorded at one of two sites counts 1/2), a biased estimator listed only as a metric. "Corrupt peers only push m(P) up" is safe for privacy but lets a corrupt peer zero out chosen patients' contributions (utility poisoning).
3. *Noise calibrated to honest parties*: relies on a fixed t chosen in advance; if more than t hospitals are corrupt the effective rho silently degrades to about rho(|S|-t)/(|S|-t') and the ledger cannot check t. A corrupt hospital in S can silently poison any answer (masked shares carry no range/validity proof; the design concedes "correctness only").
4. *Unanimous-abstain refund*: safe, but one corrupt or crashed hospital in S (S chosen by the analyst) both blocks the refund and burns the debit by never posting. P3 "availability" only says a debit succeeds, not that an answer is released. Abstain-or-post is a public signal of local state (the design notes this side channel).
5. *Per-issuing-authority reservations*: isolate authorities from each other only; inside one authority several GIDs can exhaust its reservation (sum of GID caps not bounded by it); the sponsor is chosen by the analyst from its valid issuers; the reservation set is a governance parameter changed via `majorityConsent`, the same domain-only voting confirmed in T9(a)3-4, so a sham CA obtains its own reservation (links to Candidate C); sum of reservations <= B caps the number of authorities (5 at 20%).
6. *Pairwise-mask aggregation posted on-chain*: masks hide an honest hospital's value only if at least 2 honest hospitals are in S (|S|-t >= 2); with |S|-t = 1 the honest share x_h + eta_h is exposed and only local noise protects it. Shares are public on-chain forever and mask keys are static ECDH keys without forward secrecy, so a later key leak unmasks x_h + eta_h, which carries only 1/(|S|-t) of the required variance (helper reasoning, not in the design). No dropout recovery: one missing share makes the sum unusable (see item 4).
**Bottom line for the LEAD**: the arithmetic is right under its hidden assumptions; the real risk is the one the design already names (accuracy collapse): about 225 linked queries in total for ALL analysts at a 1000-count and eps=2, and 4-9 queries if cells are small or |S|=9,t=4. Items 1, 3, 4 and 6 are places where a reviewer can break the soundness story, not just the utility story.
